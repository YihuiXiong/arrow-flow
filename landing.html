HOW TO PLAY / 01

One screen. Many drills.

The screen supplies the cue. You connect it to a direction, a target or a familiar action. Explore the examples here before opening a session.

01

Place the screen

Keep it visible from your base, outside your movement path. A cone, floor mark or a single step is enough.

02

Choose the meaning

Start with direction or number alone. Add color or a repeat rule once that mapping feels clear.

03

Move and recover

Complete the response and return to base. Set a wait long enough to finish both.

02

Change the rule, keep the cues.

Color chooses the destination

Match the palette to colored targets. Let color choose where to go and the number choose what to do there, such as 1 = touch and 2 = shadow a stroke. Keep one mapping for the whole set.

One cue means hold

Choose a number or color that means stay at base, such as 5 or yellow. Follow normal rules for other cues. Reassess each new cue; add only one exception to begin.

Follow the opposite direction

Use a familiar direction pair: a right arrow means left, and left means right. Agree on front and back in your actual space before adding them.

Read, call, move with a partner

One person reads and calls the cue; the other follows the call. Swap next set. A partner can also observe wrong choices or missed cues; the app does not score them.

No equipment? Assign numbers to familiar actions on the spot. If color is hard to distinguish, use direction or number alone. These are practical drill ideas, not a validated training dose.

SET YOUR SESSION / 02

Make room for the movement.

Cue duration is how long a symbol stays visible. The blank wait comes next. A new round begins when the next cue appears.

Content

Choose 2, 4 or 8 arrow directions, or a custom number range (1–5 by default). Arrow colors and number backgrounds have separate editable palettes.

Pace

Begin with Easy, then adjust cue duration and wait. Fixed waits are predictable; random waits vary cue onset. Neither removes the need for recovery time.

Rounds & rest

Rounds count shown cues. Sets repeat that block, with optional rest and a final three-second countdown. The bottom bar shows the current set.

Sound & screen

Sound marks cue onset, not a direction or destination. Enable it with a tap when needed. Use the corner icon for fullscreen where supported.

ABOUT ARROW FLOW / 03

A small tool for cue-based practice.

Arrow Flow turns a screen into a source of visual prompts for individual or partner drills. It works with the space, equipment and familiar movements you already have.

01

A cue appears

Direction, number, color.

02

You apply the rule

Pick the information that matters.

03

You make the move

Respond, then recover to base.

What you can vary

Read one feature or combine two. Change the cue-to-action mapping. Remember the previous cue, or withhold a movement on a chosen signal. These change what the task asks of you.

What the tool does

It presents cues and manages the session. People apply the rules. It does not track movement, judge correct responses, measure reaction time or assess brain function.

THE SCIENCE

Cue response and sport anticipation are different tasks.

3

Here: an explicit prompt

The symbol supplies a direction or target. You select a response using the rule.

In play: information from the opponent

Body and racket movement, flight and rally context help a player anticipate what happens next. A random symbol does not reproduce those cues.

Use this as a supplementary drill tool. For reading an opponent and anticipating play, keep representative practice such as partner feeds, rallies and sport-specific video work.

01Expertise and prediction

A tennis/volleyball fMRI study found expertise-related activity during sport anticipation. It was an association, not a test of a visual-cue app.

Balser et al. · 2014
02Information and context

Tennis training with rally-sequence footage benefited overall on-court decision time relative to isolated-shot footage; not every outcome differed. The training included opponent information absent here.

Broadbent et al. · 2017
03Transfer needs to be tested

A badminton study reported gains on several video and simulated-movement tasks, but no significant training effects on a match-video task. That task was not live match performance.

Wang et al. · 2025

Arrow Flow has not been validated in a controlled efficacy study. The examples above describe task designs, not proven improvements in general cognition, brain function or match performance.
